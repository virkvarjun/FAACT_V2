Chapter 2

FAACT v1 — detect risk, pick a safer chunk

The first attempt. It measured zero successes out of twenty, and the reason was not the idea.

The plan

If a chunked policy is blind while executing, then give it a monitor. Predict when the current trajectory is heading for failure, and when it is, replace the chunk with a safer alternative.

  1. Train the policy. An action-chunking policy on the ALOHA transfer-cube task. (The checkpoint referred to as "100K" is 100,000 training steps — this is a training-length figure, not a number of rollouts.)
  2. Train a monitor. A small MLP over the policy's internal features, predicting failure_within_k — "will this episode fail within the next k steps?"
  3. Generate candidate chunks at runtime. Since ACT is deterministic, you cannot just sample it repeatedly. v1 manufactured variety by perturbing the observation and by MC-dropout — running the network with dropout active so each pass differs.
  4. Score and select. Run every candidate chunk through the monitor and execute whichever is predicted least likely to fail.

The shape of this is reasonable and it mirrors published work: detect, then intervene. The failure is in the details.

What it measured

ConditionResult
Baseline, no intervention0 / 20
Monitor running, no intervention0 / 20
Best intervention configuration1 / 10
Recoveries after an intervention0, in every run

A 0% baseline is the number to stare at. If the policy never succeeds even when undisturbed, there is no headroom: no intervention can demonstrate an improvement over a floor of zero, and every subsequent measurement is uninformative.

The four root causes

1. The disturbances were never implemented

There was a --perturbation_mode flag. It was an unused placeholder. Nothing was ever disturbed. So the "failures" being detected were just a weak policy failing on its own, not recoverable incidents — there was nothing to recover from, which is why recoveries were zero by construction.

2. No headroom

With a 0% baseline, every comparison is measuring noise against zero. The experiment could not have produced a positive result even if the method were perfect.

3. The monitor predicted the wrong thing

failure_within_k means "am I near the end of an episode that failed". That is proximity to termination, not recoverability. It fires when things are already over, which is exactly when intervening cannot help. The quantity you actually want is "would intervening still help?" — which is what v2 tries to measure.

4. The candidate chunks were not useful

Perturbing the input of a deterministic policy produces chunks that are all small variations on the same motion. They do not span the space of recovery behaviours — if the correct response is "let go, back off, re-approach", no amount of jittering the observation will produce it. v1's own conclusion was that the bottleneck was action usefulness, not action scoring.

What to take from it

  • A flag that does nothing looks exactly like a flag that works. The pipeline ran, produced numbers, wrote videos, and reported metrics — all while the central experimental manipulation was absent.
  • Check the baseline before believing anything downstream. 0/20 should have halted the project on day one rather than being reported alongside intervention results.
  • Selecting among bad options is not an intervention. If none of your candidates can recover, a perfect scorer changes nothing.
  • The repository could not be run. The runtime loop lived in an untracked package and a submodule was pinned to a commit that did not exist upstream, so a fresh clone could not reproduce anything.

Every one of these became a design constraint in v2: disturbances are asserted to change the world, the baseline is verified against a reference implementation episode by episode, the target quantity is recoverability rather than proximity to failure, and every acceptance check is fixed before the run.
