Chapter 2

FAACT v1 — detect risk, pick a safer chunk

The first attempt measured zero successes out of twenty. The reason was not the idea — it was that the experiment's central manipulation was never implemented, and nothing in the pipeline noticed.

2.1  The reasoning

From chapter 1: a chunked policy is blind for 100 steps at a time. The natural fix is a monitor — a second model that watches for trouble — plus an intervention for when the monitor fires.

This mirrors a real literature. Failure prediction for robot policies is an active area, and "detect, then intervene" is the standard shape. v1's specific bet was that if you can generate several candidate chunks and score them, you can pick a safer one before committing.

2.2  The pipeline in detail

  1. Train the policy. An action-chunking policy on ALOHA transfer-cube. The checkpoint referred to as "100K" is 100,000 gradient steps of training — a training-length figure, not a count of rollouts. (The committed evaluation numbers in v1 were actually produced by a π0 policy rather than ACT, despite the write-up describing ACT throughout.)
  2. Generate labels for a failure predictor. Roll out episodes, record which ones fail, and label each timestep with failure_within_k: will this episode fail within the next k steps? This gives a binary target at every step of every episode.
  3. Train the monitor. A small MLP over features extracted from the policy's internals — the same kind of forward-hook features v2 later reuses — mapping state to predicted risk.
  4. Manufacture candidate chunks at runtime. Here is the subtle part. ACT is deterministic at inference (chapter 1.3: the CVAE latent is zeroed), so you cannot get variety by sampling it repeatedly. v1 produced variety two ways:
    • Observation perturbation — jitter the input image or state slightly and re-run, so each pass sees a marginally different world.
    • MC-dropout — keep dropout active at inference (p=0.1) instead of disabling it, so each forward pass randomly drops different units and produces a slightly different chunk. This is a standard trick for extracting uncertainty estimates from a deterministic network.
  5. Score and select. Push every candidate through the monitor, take the one with the lowest predicted risk, execute it. When the monitor's risk crossed a threshold, this substitution was triggered as an "intervention".

Nothing here is unreasonable. The components are individually sensible and the overall shape matches published approaches. It still produced nothing, for four separate reasons.

2.3  What it measured

A 0% baseline invalidates everything downstream. If the policy never succeeds even undisturbed, no intervention can demonstrate an improvement over a floor of zero. Every subsequent comparison is measuring noise. This number alone should have halted the project before any intervention was evaluated.

2.4  The four root causes

1 · The disturbances were never implemented

There was a command-line flag, --perturbation_mode. It was parsed, stored, and never used. Nothing was ever disturbed in any run.

So every "failure" the monitor was detecting was simply a weak policy failing on its own — not a recoverable incident. There was nothing to recover from, which makes "0 recoveries" true by construction rather than a finding.

The insidious part: the pipeline ran to completion, wrote videos, computed metrics and produced a results table. A flag that does nothing looks exactly like a flag that works.

2 · No headroom

Related but distinct. Even with working disturbances, a policy at 0% unperturbed has nowhere to go. Measuring an intervention requires a baseline that sometimes succeeds and sometimes does not, so that a method has room to move the number in either direction.

v2's equivalent requirement became an explicit gate: the undisturbed policy must clear a threshold before anything else runs, and disturbances must land the success rate in a band — not at the floor.

3 · The monitor predicted the wrong quantity

failure_within_k asks "am I near the end of an episode that is going to fail?". Read that carefully — it is a measure of proximity to termination, not of recoverability.

Those come apart badly. A state can be:

  • Far from failure but already doomed — the cube has been knocked out of reach at step 50; the episode will not end for another 300 steps, so the label says "safe".
  • Near failure but perfectly recoverable — the timer is running out but the robot is holding the cube correctly.

A monitor trained on this fires when the episode is nearly over, which is exactly when intervening cannot help. The quantity you want is "would intervening still change the outcome?" — which is what v2 defines as reversibility and measures directly.

4 · The candidate chunks could not recover

This is the deepest one. Perturbing the inputs of a deterministic network produces chunks that are small variations on the same motion. MC-dropout does the same: it samples around the model's existing behaviour, not away from it.

But recovery is usually a qualitatively different motion. If the correct response to a dropped cube is "open the gripper, back off, re-approach from another angle", no amount of jittering the observation will produce that — it is not a nearby point in the space the policy already covers. The candidates do not span the recovery manifold.

v1's own conclusion, and the sharpest sentence in its write-up: "the bottleneck is action usefulness, not just action scoring." A perfect scorer over a set of useless options still selects a useless option.

2.5  What v1 taught the rebuild

v1 failurev2 countermeasure
Disturbance flag silently did nothingevery disturbance object tracks whether it fired; the runner raises if an episode finishes without it firing
0% baseline was reported, not acted onbaseline is a blocking gate, verified seed for seed against the reference implementation
Wrong target quantityreversibility is defined operationally and measured by replaying the simulator, not inferred from a proxy label
Candidates could not recoverstop trying to pick a better action; change how long the policy commits instead
Repo could not be run at allno submodules, pinned dependencies, and a setup check that must print PASS before anything else runs

The most useful thing v1 produced was its own post-mortem. The four causes above are more informative than a marginal success would have been, and the arc "measured zero → diagnosed why → rebuilt around the diagnosis" is the spine of the whole project.
