Chapter 5

v2 — results, and why it fails

Reversibility turned out to be measurable and predictable. Gating the commitment horizon on it gained nothing. The reason is a cliff, and the cliff is a stall.

5.1  The result you can watch

Same seed, same disturbance, same policy. The only difference is how many steps of each predicted chunk the robot commits to before replanning — 5 on the left, 40 on the right.

Why short horizons stall

An ACT chunk is a trajectory starting from where the robot is now, and the actions are absolute joint positions (chapter 1). The first few entries of any chunk are therefore close to the current pose — the trajectory has barely departed yet.

Commit only the first 5 of them and the robot moves a tiny amount. Replan, and the policy sees a state almost identical to the one it just saw, so it emits an almost identical chunk. Take its first 5 actions again. The loop advances at a fraction of normal speed and the episode times out.

MEASURED — net joint displacement over a full episode:

  h=5    0.16      per-seed: 0.15  0.17  0.16  0.17  0.16   ← stalls every time
  h=10   1.03      per-seed: 2.03  0.16  0.16  1.57  1.21   ← bimodal, right at the edge
  h=20   1.95      per-seed: 2.05  2.07  2.03  1.53  2.09
  h=100  2.02      per-seed: 2.02  2.04  1.93  2.05  2.03

h=5 stalls on every seed tested. h=10 stalls on some and not others. h≥20 always moves. That maps precisely onto the success curve below.

This corrects an earlier belief. The intuitive story — "frequent replanning makes the motion jerky and it tears itself apart" — is wrong. Total joint path is in fact slightly higher at h=10 than h=100 (6.53 vs 5.37), so there is some extra jitter, but at h=5 the dominant effect is stalling. Nobody noticed until the rollout videos were actually watched.

Below ~20 steps: catastrophe. Above ~20 steps: nothing. The cliff comparisons are the only results in this entire project that survive correction for multiple testing (p = 10−5). Every comparison within the plateau is p ≥ 0.09.

5.3  Why that kills the method

Every gated controller built in this project — learned and oracle alike — operated at a mean horizon between 41 and 56. That is squarely inside the plateau.

A controller can only lose by approaching the cliff, and cannot gain by moving around within the flat.

This holds regardless of what the horizon is gated on — perfect information included. Which is precisely why an oracle handed ground-truth reversibility performs no better than committing blindly to 100 steps. The signal is fine. The actuator has no authority in the region where any sensible controller operates.

5.6  How the comparisons were tested

McNemar, because the conditions are paired

Every condition runs the same episodes with the same disturbances. Treating 36/76 vs 30/76 as two independent proportions throws that pairing away and loses power. McNemar's test looks only at episodes where the two disagreed:

x = episodes A won and B lost
y = episodes B won and A lost
episodes where both agreed are discarded — they carry no information

under the null:   x ~ Binomial(x + y, 0.5)

So "2 vs 12, p=0.013" means: of the 14 episodes where they differed, one condition won 12.

Why nothing survives correction

Six controllers give 15 pairwise comparisons. At α=0.05, running 15 tests yields an expected 0.75 false positives even if every condition were identical. Bonferroni demands p < 0.05/15 = 0.0033.

Only the cliff results clear that. Every reversibility-related result sits between 0.013 and 1.000 and does not.

In regime A the disturbances hit while the robot was still reaching through free space — recoverable, and there a controller that shortened selectively could help. In regime B they hit during manipulation, where three quarters of disturbed states are already lost. Once a state is unrecoverable no horizon policy can save it, so the intervention has nothing to act on and only its cost remains.

A calibration warning that generalises. Both regimes sit inside the 25–65% perturbed-success band normally used to tune disturbance strength, and they behave oppositely. Calibrating disturbances by success rate does not guarantee a useful distribution of recoverable states — the band belongs on the fraction of states that are still saveable.

5.8  Conclusion

Reversibility is measurable, predictable, and useless as a horizon controller on this task.

Right signal, wrong actuator. Three separate lines of evidence say the estimate was never the bottleneck: accuracy improved 0.479 → 0.660 → 0.693 across three datasets with control performance unmoved; calibrating the estimate made things 37% worse; and an oracle with perfect information gains nothing either.

If low reversibility should trigger anything, it is not "replan more often". It would have to be a genuinely different recovery behaviour — which is what the cut LoRA-adapter idea was for, and remains the obvious next thing to try.

5.9  Limits

  • One task, one policy, one simulator. Whether the plateau exists for other chunked policies, or for tasks with different time constants, is untested.
  • Underpowered. Outside the cliff, nothing survives multiple-comparison correction.
  • Two of four disturbance kinds do not work here, so the suite is honestly two kinds.
  • The oracle is a ceiling, not an achievable result — it reads labels measured on the very episodes it runs.
  • No error bars across seeds. Single seed set per run, as the plan specified.
  • The GPU path is untested. Everything ran on laptop CPU; the code is device-agnostic but CUDA was never exercised.
