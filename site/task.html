Chapter 1

The task, the robot, and the data

Everything downstream is measured on one task. Knowing exactly what it is — including how success is defined at the contact level — makes the rest of these notes concrete instead of abstract.

1.1  ALOHA bimanual transfer-cube

Two 6-DoF ViperX 300S arms face each other across a table. A red cube sits somewhere on the right side. The robot must pick it up with the right arm, lift it clear of the table, hand it to the left arm, and hold it there.

It is a genuinely bimanual task: neither arm can complete it alone, and the handover requires both to be in the right place at the same time. That handover is where most failures happen.

"Absolute positions" is the single most load-bearing detail on this page. It is why short commitment horizons cause the policy to stall rather than thrash — see chapter 5.

1.2  How success is defined, exactly

Reward is not shaped or hand-tuned — it is read off the physics engine's contact list. At each step the simulator reports every pair of touching geometries, and the task checks three booleans:

touch_right_gripper = ("red_box", "vx300s_right/10_right_gripper_finger") in contacts
touch_left_gripper  = ("red_box", "vx300s_left/10_left_gripper_finger")  in contacts
touch_table         = ("red_box", "table")                                in contacts

From which the stage follows:

RewardConditionIn words
0—still approaching
1right gripper touching cubecontact made
2right gripper touching, table not touchinglifted clear
3left gripper touching cubehandover attempted
4left gripper touching, table not touchingtransfer complete — success

An episode counts as a success if it ever reaches reward 4. The environment terminates immediately when it does, which is why successful episodes are shorter than failed ones (typically 230–320 steps against the full 400).

When each stage happens — measured

Timing the stages over five successful episodes gave a fact that turned out to matter enormously:

first contact with cube (reward ≥1):   median step 154
transfer complete       (reward  4):   median step 278

The original disturbance window in v2 was steps 40–160 — which means nearly every disturbance fired before the cube was even grasped. Half the perturbation suite was consequently measuring nothing. See chapter 6.

1.3  What varies between episodes

Only one thing: where the cube starts. Both arms always begin from the same home pose. The cube is sampled uniformly from a rectangle:

x ~ Uniform(0.0, 0.2)      # 20 cm range
y ~ Uniform(0.4, 0.6)      # 20 cm range
z = 0.05                   # always on the table
orientation = identity     # never rotated

A 20 × 20 cm spawn region, seeded per episode. This is what makes one seed harder than another, and it is why quoting a success rate without saying which seeds is meaningless — a fact that caused a genuine scare early in this project, described in chapter 4.

A 2 cm cube displacement — the calibrated object_displace disturbance — is a tenth of the spawn range. Small, but enough to drop success by 23 points when it lands during the grasp.

1.4  The demonstration data

episodes50
frames20,000
per episode400
rate50 Hz

lerobot/aloha_sim_transfer_cube_human — 50 human teleoperated demonstrations, 20,000 frames total. Each frame stores the overhead camera image, the 14-d joint state, and the 14-d action the human commanded.

Fifty demonstrations is not much. That scarcity is exactly the regime where compounding error bites hardest, and therefore where action chunking earns its keep — which is the subject of chapter 2.

The word human in the dataset name matters. These are teleoperated by a person, so they contain pauses, corrections and hesitation — the multimodality that makes step-level behaviour cloning stall.

1.5  The policy checkpoint used throughout

lerobot/act_aloha_sim_transfer_cube_human — an ACT policy trained on the dataset above for 80,000 steps. Rather than retrain, this project verified the published checkpoint reproduces its reference evaluation episode by episode.
