The whole project, in order

Walkthrough

The other chapters are organised by topic. This one is organised by what happened, when — including the wrong turns, because the order in which things were discovered is most of the story.

Stage 0  ·  Starting from a failure

The project begins with a post-mortem, not a hypothesis. v1 had measured 0/20 baseline, 1/10 best intervention, zero recoveries. The diagnosis was that the disturbance flag had never been wired up, so nothing was ever disturbed.

That single fact set the constraints for everything after it: verify the disturbance changes the world, verify the baseline against a reference, fix every acceptance check before the run. Details in chapter 3.

The first real decision was to not retrain ACT. The published checkpoint was tried first, on the theory that verification is cheaper than training. It loaded, and the six hours of training that had been the plan's biggest schedule risk evaporated.

Stage 1  ·  Making the environment work

Two blockers, both anticipated from v1's notes and both real:

  • Headless MuJoCo rendering. Fails by returning an all-black image that otherwise looks like success. The check therefore asserts the rendered frame has standard deviation > 1.0 rather than merely that rendering returned something.
  • Video decoding. The dataset stores camera streams as video, and the decoder needs FFmpeg shared libraries — a CLI binary on PATH is not enough. On macOS it also needs DYLD_LIBRARY_PATH, because the decoder's search path resolves against the Python prefix rather than Homebrew's.

Gate result: 6/6 checks pass. The rendered frame was also inspected by eye — two arms and a red cube — because "std > 1.0" would also pass on noise.

Stage 2  ·  A scare, and the verification that resolved it

The baseline measured 65%. The model card advertises 83%. That is a large enough gap to suspect a broken inference path.

Rather than guess, the checkpoint repository turned out to ship eval_info.json — 500 per-episode records from the reference evaluation, each tagged with its seed. Our runner uses the same seeds, so the comparison could be made episode by episode:

seeds 1000-1019 (n=20):   ours 13/20 = 65.0%   reference 13/20 = 65.0%   agreement 20/20
seeds 1000-1049 (n=50):   ours 38/50 = 76.0%   reference 38/50 = 76.0%   agreement 48/50

reference over its own first 20 seeds:   65.0%
reference over all 500 seeds:            83.0%

The reference implementation scores 65% on those same 20 seeds. The gap was the seed window, not a defect — those particular cube spawn positions are a hard draw.

The lasting consequence: at this success rate, n=20 carries a ±11 point binomial band. Every rate quoted in this project from here on states its seed window and its n, and the ablation was never run at n=20.

Stage 3  ·  Building disturbances, and two bugs in the foundations

The perturbation suite and the snapshot/restore layer were written before any policy was involved, since neither needs one. Writing the tests first paid immediately:

The bitwise reproducibility test failed

Two branches restored from an identical snapshot, driven by identical actions, diverged by 2×10−8 within 150 steps. The cause was the constraint solver's warm-start cache, absent from dm_control's 45-number state. Switching to MuJoCo's own 262-number integration state fixed it.

Then a second, worse one, found later

The episode step counter lives in a gym wrapper, not in MuJoCo. Branch 0 ran 280 steps; branches 1 and 2 ran one step each, silently. Both are described in chapter 6.

Neither would have surfaced from looking at results. The first was caught by a test demanding bitwise equality; the second by a test asserting equal step budgets.

Stage 4  ·  Measuring reversibility for the first time

With snapshot/restore trustworthy, the first labelled episode produced exactly the signature the project was built to look for:

seed 2001, grasp_slip, disturbance at step 42

t:    25    50    75   100   125   150   175   200 ...
R:  1.00  0.38  0.88  0.88  1.00  0.88  0.75  1.00 ...
            ↑
       disturbance lands — recoverability collapses, then recovers

R drops sharply when the disturbance lands and climbs back as the policy recovers. Reversibility was behaving like a real quantity.

Stage 5  ·  The first model, and a false diagnosis

At 40 labelled episodes the model reached ρ = 0.479, below the 0.50 gate. Three hypotheses were available:

  1. The labels are too noisy. Tested by measuring the labels' own test-retest reliability: 0.977. Ruled out — more branches would buy nothing.
  2. Too many features for the sample size. 1206 dimensions against ~300 training states. A feature sweep showed the 1024-d transformer features were the worst performer while fitting training data best — the signature of overfitting.
  3. Not enough data. Implied by the other two.

Doubling the dataset moved ρ from 0.479 to 0.660, confirming the diagnosis.

But a conclusion drawn along the way was wrong. At 40 episodes the 28-dimensional feature set beat the 1024-d one, and this was written up as "reversibility is predictable from arm state alone; the policy's internals add nothing". At 80 episodes that reversed. It was a sample-size effect, not a fact about features — the first of four conclusions in this project to reverse under a better test.

Stage 6  ·  The ablation, and a result that did not replicate

The first ablation at n=30 showed the gated controller leading: 57% against 50% and 53%. Encouraging — and not significant.

Doubling to n=60 reversed the ordering: gated fell to 48% while fixed h=20 rose to 58%. The n=30 lead had been noise. Had the project stopped there, it would have reported a win that was not real.

Then the oracle appeared to rescue it

The decisive experiment is the oracle: the same controller fed ground-truth measured R instead of a prediction. It separates "reversibility is the wrong variable" from "our estimate is too weak".

oracle vs fixed h=100:   77% vs 65%   p = 0.013
oracle vs fixed h=20:    77% vs 61%   p = 0.019

The first significant result in the project. The reading at the time: the lever works, our estimator is the bottleneck.

Stage 7  ·  Discovering the disturbances were mistimed

A calibration sweep — run to close a gap that had been marked as a known weakness rather than to chase a suspicion — found that grasp_slip scored 65% at every duration, identical to undisturbed. It was doing nothing.

Measuring the task's own timing explained it:

first cube contact:        median step 154
transfer complete:         median step 278
disturbance window in use:        40-160     ← almost entirely before the grasp

Forcing a gripper open before it is holding anything does nothing. Roughly half the labelled dataset consisted of episodes whose disturbance had no effect. The window moved to 150–280 and the whole dataset was relabelled.

The acceptance check that should have caught this was itself flawed: it tested only whether success landed in a 25–65% band, and with a 65% baseline a do-nothing disturbance passes. It now requires a 15-point drop as well.

Stage 8  ·  The oracle result evaporates

Re-running on correctly-timed disturbances reversed the headline finding:

Mistimed disturbancesCorrect disturbances
fixed h=10065%47%
oracle R77% (p=0.013)39% (p=0.109)
Post-onset states already unrecoverable39%75%

With correct disturbances the oracle loses to committing blindly. Three quarters of disturbed states are already unrecoverable, so the intervention has nothing to act on and only its cost remains.

Second reversal. And a calibration warning that generalises: both regimes sat inside the standard 25–65% success band and behaved oppositely. Calibrating disturbances by success rate does not guarantee a useful distribution of recoverable states.

Stage 9  ·  The sweep that explained everything

A claim had been made from six scattered ablation points — "success is monotone in how long you commit, ρ=0.94". A direct seven-point sweep tested it properly and showed it was wrong.

A cliff and a plateau, not a ramp. Third reversal. And it explains every negative result at once: every gated controller operated at a mean horizon of 41–56, inside the plateau, where the horizon does not change success at all.

Stage 10  ·  Watching the videos, and the fourth reversal

The explanation written for the cliff was that frequent replanning makes the motion jerky and self-destructive, because ACT emits absolute joint targets. Plausible, and never checked.

Then someone watched the rollout and asked why the arm was not moving.

MEASURED — net joint displacement over an episode:
  h=5     0.16     per-seed: 0.15  0.17  0.16  0.17  0.16
  h=100   2.02     per-seed: 2.02  2.04  1.93  2.05  2.03

An ACT chunk begins at the current pose. Commit only its first 5 actions, replan from a barely-changed state, and the policy re-emits a near-identical chunk. The robot creeps at a fraction of speed and runs out of time.

The numbers were right the whole time; the explanation was wrong. No automated check catches that — a test can assert a value, not a sentence. It was caught by a human looking at raw output, which is the one thing the rest of the pipeline cannot do.

Where it ended

Reversibility is measurable, predictable, and useless as a horizon controller on this task.

Right signal, wrong actuator. Three independent lines of evidence say the estimate was never the bottleneck: accuracy improved 0.479 → 0.660 → 0.693 with control performance unmoved; calibrating it made things 37% worse; and an oracle with perfect information gains nothing either.

If low reversibility should trigger anything, it is not "replan more often" — the horizon has no authority in the region any controller occupies. It would have to be a genuinely different recovery behaviour.

Four of the project's own conclusions reversed under better tests — that a better estimator would help, that calibration would help, that the oracle beat the baseline, and that success was monotone in horizon. Each reversal came from running the check rather than from noticing something looked wrong. That is the process working.
