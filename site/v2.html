Chapter 3

FAACT v2 — reversibility and the commitment horizon

The rebuild. Different target quantity, different actuator, and every gate fixed in advance. It produced a clean negative result and a mechanism that explains it.

The reframe

v1 asked "will this fail?" and answered by swapping the action. v2 asks "can I still come back from here?" and answers by changing how long the policy commits.

Reversibility, defined so it can be measured

R(s) is the probability the task still succeeds if you stop and replan from state s right now. It is not estimated from a proxy — it is measured by replaying the simulator.

h = clip(h_min, 100, 100 × R̂γ)

High R: a mistake is still fixable, so commit long and keep the smooth motion. Low R: the point of no return is close, so commit short and stay reactive.

Note there is no entropy or uncertainty term here. Uncertainty asks "does the policy know what to do?" Reversibility asks "does it still matter?" — a state can be perfectly unambiguous and completely unrecoverable.

3.2  How reversibility is actually measured

This is the core primitive, and it is deliberately not a model. The simulator is asked the question directly, by being replayed.

for each perturbed episode:
    for t in 0, 25, 50, ... :
        s = snapshot(simulator)          # full state, see the traps below
        wins = 0
        for m in 1..8:                   # 8 independent branches
            restore(simulator, s)
            wins += rollout_to_end(policy, replanning from scratch)
        R[t] = wins / 8

The fraction of branches that still succeed is R at that state. No proxy, no learned surrogate, no assumption — just the simulator answering.

Trap 1: what "snapshot the simulator" has to include

The obvious call is dm_control's physics.get_state() — 45 numbers: joint positions, velocities, actuator state. It is not enough. It omits the constraint solver's warm-start cache, the previous iteration's solution that MuJoCo uses as a starting guess.

Restore without it and the solver begins from a different guess. Two branches from an identical snapshot then diverged by 2×10−8 within 150 steps, growing from there. The fix is MuJoCo's own mjSTATE_INTEGRATION — 262 numbers instead of 45. Branches are then bitwise identical, which is asserted by a test.

Trap 2: state that lives outside the simulator

The episode step counter lives in a gym wrapper, not in MuJoCo. Restoring physics leaves it running. Measured consequence, from a snapshot at step 120:

branch 0:  ran 280 steps
branch 1:  ran   1 step      ← counter already at the limit
branch 2:  ran   1 step

Every branch after the first was truncated immediately, so R would have silently collapsed to (did branch 0 win?) / 8 — a number that looks like a probability and is not one. Nothing raised an error. The snapshot now carries the counter too, and a test asserts all branches get equal step budgets.

Trap 3: a deterministic policy makes 8 branches pointless

Restore is now bitwise exact and ACT is deterministic (chapter 1.3). So all 8 branches would replay identically, and R could only ever be 0 or 1 — a "probability" welded to one side.

Each branch therefore adds small Gaussian noise to its actions. The scale has to be chosen carefully: large enough to spread outcomes, small enough that it does not itself change what the policy can do. Measured on 20 unperturbed episodes:

noise 0.0   → 13/20 = 65%    identical to baseline (rollout path is faithful)
noise 0.01  → 13/20 = 65%    ← chosen
noise 0.02  →  8/20 = 40%    already destructive; would bias every label down
noise 0.05  →  0/20 =  0%

Trap 4: measurement noise could have been the whole story

R comes from only 8 branches, so it carries binomial error of its own. Before blaming the model for a mediocre fit, this was checked: treat the measured R as the true probability, draw two independent re-measurements with 8 branches each, and see how well they agree.

They correlate at 0.977 — because 86% of R values sit at exactly 0 or 1, where re-measurement is nearly deterministic. So label noise was not the limit, and raising the branch count would have bought nothing. That single check redirected effort toward more episodes instead of more branches, which is what actually improved the model.

3.3  Methodology, step by step

  1. Get a working baseline and prove it. Rather than checking against a threshold, the policy was compared seed for seed against the reference implementation's published 500-episode evaluation. Result: 48/50 episodes agree, 76.0% vs 76.0%. Chunk predictions are bitwise identical to the reference inference path.
  2. Build disturbances that provably do something. Four kinds — teleport the cube, force the grippers open, add noise to joint commands, black out the camera. Each is swept across magnitudes, and the acceptance test requires both a success rate in 25–65% and a drop of at least 15 points versus undisturbed.
  3. Label reversibility by branch rollout. At every 25th step: snapshot the full simulator state, then 8 times over — restore it, let the policy replan from scratch, run to the end, and record whether it succeeded. The fraction that succeed is R at that state.
  4. Train a model to predict R from the policy's internal features, evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation split by episode — never within one, since states 25 steps apart are near-duplicates.
  5. Gate the horizon on it and compare against fixed horizons and against an oracle that is handed the true measured R.

Below ~20 steps the arm stalls and the policy collapses. Above it, the horizon does not measurably change anything — rank correlation with success is +0.10 for h ≥ 20. Every gated controller operated at a mean horizon of 41–56, squarely inside the plateau.

So a controller can only lose by approaching the cliff, and cannot gain by moving around within the flat — no matter what it gates on. Which is exactly why an oracle handed ground-truth reversibility does no better than a fixed horizon.

3.7  How the comparisons were tested

Every condition runs the same episodes with the same disturbances, so the outcomes are paired. That matters for which test is appropriate.

McNemar rather than a two-proportion test

Comparing 36/76 against 30/76 as two independent proportions throws away the pairing and loses power. McNemar's test looks only at the episodes where the two conditions disagreed:

episodes where A won and B lost:   x
episodes where B won and A lost:   y
episodes where both agreed:        ignored — they carry no information

under the null hypothesis, x ~ Binomial(x+y, 0.5)

So "oracle vs fixed h=100: 2 vs 12, p=0.013" means: of the 14 episodes where they differed, the oracle won 12. Agreements are discarded because an episode both controllers win tells you nothing about which is better.

Why nothing here survives correction

Six controllers means 15 pairwise comparisons. Running 15 tests at α=0.05 means an expected 0.75 false positives even if every condition were identical. A Bonferroni correction demands p < 0.05/15 = 0.0033.

Only the cliff results clear that bar (p = 0.00001). Every reversibility-related result — including the oracle win that briefly looked like a positive finding — sits between 0.013 and 1.000 and does not survive.

The experiment is underpowered, and by how much

Given the measured rate of disagreement between conditions (~40% of episodes), the episodes needed per condition to detect a true difference with 80% power:

In regime A, disturbances hit while the robot was still reaching through free space — recoverable, and a controller that shortened selectively could help. In regime B they hit during manipulation, where three quarters of disturbed states are already lost. Once a state is unrecoverable, no horizon policy can save it, so the intervention has nothing to act on and only its cost remains.

A methodological warning that generalises. Both regimes sit inside the 25–65% perturbed-success band normally used to calibrate disturbance strength, and they behave oppositely. Calibrating disturbances by success rate does not guarantee a useful distribution of recoverable states — the band belongs on the fraction of states that are still saveable.

The conclusion

Reversibility is measurable, predictable, and useless as a horizon controller on this task.

Right signal, wrong actuator. If low reversibility should trigger anything, it is not "replan more often" — it would have to be a genuinely different recovery behaviour, which is what the cut LoRA-adapter idea was for.

Limits

  • One task, one policy, one simulator. Whether the plateau exists elsewhere is untested.
  • Outside the cliff results, nothing survives correction for multiple comparisons. Detecting the differences in play needs roughly 300–1250 episodes per condition; we ran 37–79.
  • Two of four disturbance kinds are ineffective here — blacking out 90% of the camera costs this policy 5 points, because it runs mostly on proprioception.
  • An oracle reads labels measured on the very episodes it runs. It bounds what is achievable; it is not itself achievable.
