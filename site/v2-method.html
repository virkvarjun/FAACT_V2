Chapter 4

v2 — measuring reversibility

What reversibility is, how it is measured rather than estimated, the four traps that had to be solved before the measurement meant anything, and how a model is trained to predict it.

4.1  The reframe

v1 asked "will this fail?" and answered by swapping the action. Both halves were wrong: the target measured proximity to termination, and the candidate actions could not recover.

v2 changes both. The question becomes "can I still come back from here?", and the response becomes how long to commit rather than what to do.

Reversibility, defined so it can be measured

R(s) = the probability the task still succeeds if the policy stops and replans from state s right now.

h = clip(h_min, 100, 100 × R̂γ)

High R → a mistake is still fixable → commit long and keep the smooth motion. Low R → the point of no return is close → commit short and stay reactive. γ controls how sharply the controller becomes cautious as R falls.

Why this is not uncertainty, and not entropy

A common instinct is to gate on the policy's uncertainty. That is a different question with a different answer.

  • Uncertainty / entropy asks: does the policy know what to do?
  • Reversibility asks: does it still matter?

These come apart in both directions. A robot that has knocked the cube off the table may be completely confident about its next action and completely doomed — low entropy, zero reversibility. A robot pausing at a genuine fork may be maximally uncertain and perfectly fine either way — high entropy, high reversibility.

There is no entropy term anywhere in this project. Reversibility is computed by replaying the simulator, not by inspecting the policy's output distribution.

4.2  The branch-rollout algorithm

This is the core primitive and it is deliberately not a model. The simulator is asked the question directly by being replayed:

for each perturbed episode:
    for t in 0, 25, 50, ..., 375:          # every 25th step
        s = snapshot(simulator)             # full state — see the traps below
        wins = 0
        for m in 1..8:                      # 8 independent branches
            restore(simulator, s)
            wins += rollout_to_end(policy, replanning from scratch)
        R[t] = wins / 8
        record(features_at_t, R[t])
    restore(simulator, s)                   # leave the episode as we found it

Cost, and the one optimisation that makes it feasible

Naively this is enormous: 80 episodes × 16 label points × 8 branches × ~250 steps ≈ 2.6 million simulator steps. The trick is that branches run open-loop at h=100, so a 250-step branch costs about 3 policy forward passes rather than 250.

MuJoCo:        ~84 steps/second
ACT forward:   ~76 ms

250-step branch  =  2.96 s of simulation  +  0.23 s of policy

The simulator is the bottleneck by roughly 13×. This is why the entire project ran on a laptop CPU: a GPU would accelerate the 0.23 s and leave the 2.96 s untouched. What matters is performance-core count, not an accelerator.

4.3  Four traps in "just snapshot and replay"

The algorithm above is four lines of pseudocode and every one of them hides a way to produce numbers that look like probabilities and are not.

Trap 1 · What "snapshot the simulator" must include

The obvious call is dm_control's physics.get_state() — 45 numbers covering joint positions, velocities and actuator state. It is not enough.

MuJoCo's constraint solver is iterative, and it starts each step from the previous step's solution — a warm-start cache. That cache is not in the 45 numbers. Restore without it and the solver begins from a different initial guess, producing a slightly different answer.

physics.get_state()        45 numbers   → branches diverge by 2e-8 within 150 steps
mjSTATE_INTEGRATION       262 numbers   → branches bitwise identical

Two branches from an identical snapshot must be bitwise identical when driven by identical actions, and a test asserts exactly that. Approximate agreement is not good enough: the divergence compounds, and every label would inherit the error.

Trap 2 · State that lives outside the simulator

The episode step counter is not in MuJoCo at all — it lives in a gym TimeLimit wrapper. Restoring physics leaves it running. Measured from a snapshot taken at step 120:

branch 0:  ran 280 steps
branch 1:  ran   1 step     ← counter already at the 400 limit
branch 2:  ran   1 step

Every branch after the first was truncated immediately. R would have silently collapsed to (did branch 0 win?) / 8 — which can only take the values 0 or 0.125, looks like a probability, and is not one. Nothing raised an error.

The snapshot now carries the counter alongside the physics state, and a test asserts every branch receives an equal step budget.

Trap 3 · A deterministic policy makes 8 branches pointless

Having made restore bitwise exact, a new problem appears. ACT is deterministic at inference (chapter 2: the CVAE latent is zeroed). So all 8 branches replay identically and R can only ever be 0 or 1 — a "probability" welded to one side, and 8× the compute for no information.

Each branch therefore adds small Gaussian noise to its actions, seeded per branch. R becomes the success probability under the policy's own execution variability — which is closer to what a controller actually needs to know.

The noise scale is a real trade-off: large enough to spread outcomes, small enough not to change what the policy can do. Measured on 20 unperturbed episodes:

noise 0.0    → 13/20 = 65%    identical to baseline — confirms the rollout path is faithful
noise 0.01   → 13/20 = 65%    ← chosen
noise 0.02   →  8/20 = 40%    already destructive; would bias every label downward
noise 0.05   →  0/20 =  0%    completely breaks the policy

Note the 0.0 row does double duty: matching the baseline exactly proves the branch-rollout code path executes the policy the same way normal evaluation does.

Trap 4 · Ruling out measurement noise before blaming the model

R is estimated from only 8 branches, so it carries binomial error. If a model then predicts R only moderately well, is that the model's fault or the label's?

Answering it requires no new experiments: treat each measured R as the true probability, draw two independent re-measurements of 8 branches each, and see how well they agree with each other. That is the ceiling any predictor could reach.

test-retest reliability at M=8:   0.977
our model:                        0.693     → 71% of the achievable ceiling
ceiling if M=16:                  0.992
ceiling if M=32:                  0.999

The reliability is so high because 86% of R values sit at exactly 0 or 1, where re-measurement is nearly deterministic. Label noise was not the limit, so raising the branch count would have bought essentially nothing.

This single check redirected effort from "run more branches" to "label more episodes" — and doubling episodes is what actually moved the model from 0.479 to 0.693.

4.4  Building disturbances that provably do something

v1's fatal bug was a disturbance flag that did nothing. v2 implements four kinds, each hooking the episode loop at a different point:

KindHookWhat it does
object_displacephysicsteleports the cube 2 cm in a random planar direction
grasp_slipactionforces both grippers fully open for N steps
actuation_noiseactionadds Gaussian noise to joint commands over a 20-step window
occlusionobservationblacks out a rectangular patch of the camera image

The hook order within a step is deliberate and was a source of subtle error:

1. apply physics disturbance    (teleport the cube)
2. RE-OBSERVE                   ← so the policy sees the moved cube
3. apply observation disturbance (occlusion)
4. policy acts
5. apply action disturbance      (slip, noise)
6. step the simulator

Getting steps 1 and 2 backwards would teleport the cube and then let the policy act on an image of where it used to be — a disturbance the policy cannot even perceive, and one that would look like a much stronger effect than it is.

Two of the four do not work

The acceptance test requires both a success rate inside 25–65% and a drop of at least 15 points versus undisturbed. Measured against a 65% baseline:

KindBest settingSuccessDropVerdict
object_displace2 cm42%−23works
actuation_noiseσ=0.0537%−28works
grasp_slipany duration53%−12too weak
occlusion90% of the image53%−12too weak

Blacking out 90% of the only camera costs this policy 12 points. That is a finding in its own right: ACT is running almost entirely on proprioception for this task, and a visual disturbance cannot meaningfully perturb it.

4.5  Predicting R from the policy's own features

With labels in hand, a small MLP is trained to predict R from features extracted out of ACT by forward hooks — no fork of the policy required.

Feature groupDimWhat it is
feat_encoder_mean512transformer encoder output, mean-pooled over its 302 tokens
feat_decoder_mean512decoder output, mean-pooled over its 100 queries
feat_action_prefix_flat_10140the first 10 actions of the chunk, flattened
feat_state14current joint positions
feat_action_first14the very next action
feat_action_prefix_mean_1014mean of the first 10 actions

Total 1206 dimensions. The head is input → 256 → 128 → 1 with a sigmoid output, trained with soft-target binary cross-entropy against R ∈ [0,1].

Why the hooks assert they fired

A forward hook that silently produces nothing yields a vector of zeros. Train on that and the model learns to predict the mean — which looks like "these features just aren't informative" rather than like a bug. The wrapper therefore raises if a hook did not fire, and tests assert the captured vectors are non-zero, non-constant, and change when the observation changes.

Splitting by episode, never within one

States 25 steps apart in the same episode share a seed, a disturbance, and often the same action chunk. They are near-duplicates. A random split over states would put near-copies of the test set into training, inflating the score and producing a model that looks calibrated and is useless.

All splits are therefore by episode. Evaluation uses 5-fold cross-validation where every episode is held out exactly once, and predictions are pooled across folds so the reported number uses all 1,000 states rather than one arbitrary fifth of them.

A single 24/8/8 split was tried first and abandoned: it selected one feature set on validation at ρ=0.509 which then scored 0.265 on test — a 0.24 swing on the same model, purely from an 8-episode test set.
