Chapter 1

Action chunking, and why it works

Before any of the FAACT work makes sense, you need the problem action chunking solves and the price it charges.

The problem: compounding error

Behaviour cloning trains a policy to copy a human: given the robot's observation, output the action the demonstrator took. Train it with supervised learning, run it on the robot. It fails in a specific and famous way.

The training data only contains states the human visited. The moment the policy makes a small error it lands slightly off that distribution — in a state no demonstration covered. Its next prediction is now a guess, which produces a larger error, which lands it further off-distribution. Errors feed on themselves.

Why it compounds rather than averaging out

This is covariate shift: the state distribution the policy induces is not the one it was trained on, and the mismatch grows with every step. The classic analysis says that if a policy makes a mistake with probability ε per step, cloning error over a horizon of T steps grows like εT² rather than εT — quadratic, because each error also makes future errors more likely.

The key term is T — the number of decisions. Anything that reduces how many independent decisions the policy makes over an episode attacks the problem directly.

The idea: predict a chunk, not a step

Instead of predicting one action, predict the next k actions as a single unit and execute them open-loop. In ACT, k = 100 — roughly two seconds of motion at 50 Hz.

A 400-step episode is then not 400 decisions but 400 / 100 = 4. That is the whole trick: chunking cuts the effective horizon by a factor of k, and with it the compounding-error term.

It also fixes a second, less obvious problem

Human demonstrations contain pauses and hesitation. At a single timestep, a paused demonstrator looks identical whether they are about to move left, move right, or keep waiting — the same observation maps to several valid actions. A step-level policy trained on that averages them, and averaging incompatible actions often produces stopping. Policies get stuck.

Predicting a whole chunk sidesteps this: the ambiguity lives at the level of "which of several motions", and a chunk commits to one of them coherently instead of blending them into paralysis.

How ACT is actually built

ACT (Action Chunking Transformer) is a conditional VAE with a transformer inside. Numbers below are read directly off the checkpoint used in this project (lerobot/act_aloha_sim_transfer_cube_human, 51.6M parameters).

  1. Vision backbone. A ResNet-18 processes the 480×640 camera image. It downsamples by 32, giving a 15×20 feature map — 300 image tokens of 512 dimensions each.
  2. Extra tokens. The 14-dimensional joint state is projected to one token, and the CVAE latent to another. Total input to the transformer encoder: 302 tokens — verified by hooking the model and reading the tensor shape (302, 1, 512).
  3. Transformer encoder mixes those 302 tokens into a representation of the scene.
  4. Transformer decoder takes 100 learned query embeddings — one per action in the chunk — and attends over the encoder output. Its output shape is (100, 1, 512).
  5. Action head projects each of the 100 decoder outputs to a 14-dimensional joint command. The chunk is the whole 100 × 14 matrix, produced in one forward pass.

What the CVAE part is for

During training an extra encoder sees the ground-truth action chunk and compresses it into a latent variable. This gives the model somewhere to put the variability in human demonstrations, so the main network is not forced to average over it. At inference the latent is set to its prior mean (zero) — which is why ACT is deterministic at test time, a fact that matters a lot in chapter 2.

Actions are absolute joint positions, not deltas. Each entry of the chunk says "put the joints here", not "move by this much". That detail turns out to cause the single most surprising result in chapter 3.

The price: two seconds of blindness

While executing a chunk, the policy is not looking. If the cube is knocked at step 3 of a chunk, nothing can respond until step 100. Smoothness and robustness-to-compounding-error are bought with reactivity.

The ACT paper offers one mitigation, temporal ensembling: replan every step and average the overlapping predictions with exponentially decaying weights. That restores reactivity at the cost of more computation — and it is off in this project, deliberately, because the whole point is to study the commitment horizon as an explicit, controllable quantity.

Which sets up the question both FAACT versions ask

If committing is good for smoothness but bad for reactivity, the robot should presumably commit less when something has gone wrong. So: how do you know when something has gone wrong, and what should you do about it?

v1 answered: predict failure, and swap in a safer chunk.
v2 answered: measure recoverability, and shorten the commitment.
