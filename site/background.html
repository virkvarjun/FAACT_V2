Chapter 1

Action chunking, and why it works

The problem it solves, the mechanism by which it solves it, how ACT is assembled internally, and the reactivity it trades away. Every number in the architecture section was read off the actual checkpoint used in this project.

1.1  Behaviour cloning and covariate shift

Behaviour cloning is supervised learning on demonstrations. Collect pairs of (observation, action) from a human teleoperating the robot, fit π(a|o) to predict the human's action, deploy. It is the simplest possible approach and it has one deep flaw.

The train/test distribution mismatch

Supervised learning assumes training and test data come from the same distribution. In control this is false by construction. Your training states were visited by the expert. Your test states are visited by your policy. The moment the policy differs from the expert at all, it starts visiting a different distribution of states than the one it was trained on.

This is covariate shift, and it is self-reinforcing: an error moves you off-distribution, where the policy is less reliable, which produces a bigger error, which moves you further off.

Why the error is quadratic in the horizon

The standard analysis (Ross & Bagnell, the work that motivated DAgger) makes the cost precise. Suppose the policy takes a wrong action with probability at most ε on states drawn from the expert's own distribution — a very ordinary supervised-learning guarantee.

expert distribution:      error grows like   ε · T      (linear, benign)
policy's own distribution: error grows like   ε · T²     (quadratic)

The intuition for the extra factor of T: a mistake at step t does not just cost you that step. It can put you in an unfamiliar state for all remaining T−t steps. Summing that over every possible point of first failure gives the square.

The lever is T. The number of decisions is what the quadratic is quadratic in. Cut the number of decisions and you attack compounding error directly, without needing a better model, more data, or online expert correction.

1.2  Chunking: predict k actions, decide k times less often

Instead of mapping an observation to one action, map it to the next k actions and execute them open-loop. In ACT, k = 100 — about two seconds at 50 Hz.

step-by-step   400 observations → 400 actions   =  400 decisions
chunked k=100    4 observations → 400 actions   =    4 decisions

A 400-step episode becomes 4 decisions instead of 400. If compounding error scales with the number of decisions, dividing that count by 100 is a large and essentially free win. That is the primary argument for chunking.

The second problem chunking fixes: multimodality at pauses

Human demonstrations are full of hesitation. Consider a demonstrator who pauses for half a second before grasping. Across the dataset, that same paused image is labelled with many different actions — keep waiting, start reaching, adjust slightly.

A step-level policy trained with a regression loss on that data learns the average of those actions. The average of "wait" and "move" is "barely move". So the policy stalls at exactly the moments the human hesitated — a very common and very frustrating BC failure.

Chunking helps because the ambiguity is resolved once, at the level of a whole motion, rather than independently at every timestep. The policy commits to one coherent two-second motion instead of averaging incompatible micro-decisions 100 times in a row.

Keep this failure mode in mind. "Averaging produces a policy that barely moves" reappears in chapter 3 in a different guise, and it is the most surprising result in the project.

1.3  How ACT is actually built

ACT is a conditional variational autoencoder whose decoder is a transformer. Every value below was read directly off lerobot/act_aloha_sim_transfer_cube_human, the checkpoint used throughout this project.

Only one decoder layer. The original paper describes a deeper decoder, but the reference implementation uses a single layer and lerobot matches it. Worth knowing because it means most of ACT's 51.6M parameters are not in the part that generates actions.

Parameter budget

total            51.6M
  vision backbone   11.2M   ResNet-18
  transformer enc   17.3M   4 layers x 512
  VAE encoder       17.3M   4 layers x 512   (training only — discarded at inference)
  transformer dec    5.4M   1 layer

A third of the network exists only to be thrown away at test time.

The forward pass, token by token

  1. Image → tokens. ResNet-18 takes the 480×640 camera frame and downsamples by 32, giving a 15×20 spatial feature map. Flatten that and you have 300 image tokens, each projected to 512 dimensions.
  2. Proprioception → 1 token. The 14-dimensional joint state (2 arms × [6 joints + 1 gripper]) is projected to a single 512-d token.
  3. Style → 1 token. The 32-d CVAE latent is projected to one more token.
  4. Encoder input = 302 tokens. 300 + 1 + 1. Verified by attaching a forward hook and reading the tensor: (302, 1, 512). Positional embeddings are added — 2-D sinusoidal for the image grid, learned for the two 1-D tokens.
  5. Decoder = 100 learned queries. One query embedding per action in the chunk. They are learned parameters, not derived from input — the decoder's job is to turn "query #37" into "the 37th action of this motion" by cross-attending to the encoder output. Verified shape: (100, 1, 512).
  6. Action head. A linear projection maps each of the 100 decoder outputs to 14 joint commands. Output: a 100 × 14 matrix, produced in one pass.

The CVAE half, and why it exists

Section 1.2 said human demonstrations are multimodal and that averaging them is harmful. The CVAE is ACT's mechanism for handling that inside the model.

During training, a separate 4-layer transformer encoder sees the ground-truth action chunk and compresses it into a 32-dimensional latent z. The main network is then conditioned on z. This gives the model a place to store "which of the several valid ways of doing this was the human doing", so it does not have to average them away.

At inference, there is no ground-truth chunk to encode, so z is set to the prior mean — zero. The VAE encoder is discarded entirely.

Consequence: ACT is deterministic at test time. Same observation in, same chunk out, every time. This single fact is why v1's candidate sampling could not work as intended.

The training loss

L  =  L1(predicted chunk, true chunk)  +  10.0 · KL(q(z|chunk) || N(0,I))

L1 rather than L2 is a deliberate choice: L2 punishes large errors quadratically, which pushes the model harder toward averaging when the data is multimodal — precisely the failure chunking is meant to avoid. L1 tolerates committing to one mode. The KL term keeps the latent close to its prior so that zeroing it at inference is a reasonable thing to do.

1.4  The price: two seconds of blindness

Executing a 100-step chunk open-loop means the policy does not look at the world for 100 steps. Knock the cube at step 3 and nothing responds until step 100. Chunking buys smoothness and robustness to compounding error by spending reactivity.

Temporal ensembling, the paper's mitigation

ACT offers an option: replan every step, and because chunks overlap, average all the predictions that apply to the current timestep with exponentially decaying weights.

â(t)  =  Σᵢ wᵢ · aᵢ(t)   with   wᵢ ∝ exp(−m · i)

where aᵢ(t) is the prediction for time t made by the chunk generated i steps ago

This restores per-step reactivity while keeping motion smooth, at the cost of a forward pass every step instead of every 100.

It is switched off throughout this project (temporal_ensemble_coeff = None in the checkpoint), deliberately. The entire subject of FAACT is the commitment horizon as an explicit, controllable quantity, and temporal ensembling blurs what "committed for h steps" even means.

Which sets up the question both FAACT versions ask

Committing is good for smoothness and bad for reactivity. So the robot should presumably commit less when something has gone wrong. Two questions follow: how do you know something has gone wrong, and what should you do about it?

v1: predict failure, and swap in a chunk scored as safer.
v2: measure recoverability, and shorten the commitment.

v1 failed for reasons that turned out to be bugs. v2 failed for a reason that turned out to be interesting.
